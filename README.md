# Life Expectancy Index (Tableau Dashboard)

## Key Questions

- Does **higher alcohol consumption** correlate with **lower life expectancy**?
- How does **development status** shape life expectancy trends over time?
- Does **more schooling** always mean **longer life expectancy**?
- How do **GDP and infant deaths** relate, and what does that imply about healthcare access?

## Dashboard Highlights (What You’ll See)

### 1) GDP vs Infant Deaths (Heat Map)
A heat map where darker colors represent **higher infant death rates**. Several countries show that higher GDP often aligns with fewer infant deaths, but there are exceptions suggesting uneven resource distribution. 
### 2) Schooling vs Life Expectancy
A comparison of countries shows schooling often correlates with longer life expectancy, but not perfectly (e.g., some countries achieve high life expectancy with fewer schooling years).

### 3) Global Life Expectancy Map (2000–2015)
A world map with a gradient showing low life expectancy (red/orange) to high (blue). Sub-Saharan Africa trends lower, while regions like Europe/North America/Japan trend higher.
### 4) Developed vs Developing Trend Lines (2000–2015)
Both groups improve over time, but a persistent gap remains—developed countries stay roughly **10–12 years higher** throughout the period. 

### 5) Alcohol Consumption vs Life Expectancy (Bubble Chart)
High alcohol consumption does **not** show a consistent linear relationship with life expectancy—some high-consumption countries still have high life expectancy, while others do not. 

---

## Key Findings (Summary)

- **Developed nations** show consistently higher life expectancy than developing nations over 2000–2015, with steady improvement in both groups. 
- **Schooling** is related to life expectancy, but it’s not a perfect predictor—other factors (healthcare quality, diet, policy, lifestyle) matter a lot.
- **Alcohol consumption** alone doesn’t explain life expectancy differences; outcomes vary widely depending on broader socioeconomic and health-system conditions.  
- **GDP and infant deaths** often move in expected directions (more resources → fewer deaths), but country-level exceptions suggest distribution/efficiency issues.
